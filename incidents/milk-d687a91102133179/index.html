Article-level evidence

Study at Ludhiana veterinary university links contaminated cattle feed to toxins detected in milk.

Supports: Title, Summary

Article text

Cleaned research copy

Ludhiana: Consumers must prioritise milk sourced from well-managed farms, a new study warns, after research conducted at Guru Angad Dev Veterinary and Animal Sciences University (Gadvasu) found that improperly stored cattle feed in the unorganized dairy sector can lead to harmful levels of aflatoxins in milk. Aflatoxins, which are classified as Group 1 human carcinogens, pose a serious health risk, particularly to infants and children. Tejbeer Singh, a PhD scholar in Animal Nutrition at Gadvasu, presented the findings during a "One Health" symposium at the university. He advised consumers to check the source of their milk because a lack of awareness among small dairy farmers can lead to the development of fungal moulds in feed, resulting in contamination. Study Finds High Contamination in Feed The study, titled "Impact of Farm Size and Seasonal Variation on Aflatoxin Contamination and Nutritional Value of Maize and Wheat in Punjab," analyzed 42 samples of maize and wheat (21 of each) collected between January and September across four districts in Punjab. Samples were taken from small, medium, and large farms to track seasonal and scale-based variations. Key Findings More than 80% of maize samples and approximately 38% of wheat samples tested positive for aflatoxins. Aflatoxin B1 was the primary toxin detected. Contamination was highest during the rainy season and the winter. Small farms showed a significantly higher concentration of Aflatoxin B1 in the feed. The researcher noted that maize consistently showed higher Aflatoxin B1 contamination than wheat. The study also indicated that seasonal variation and farm size significantly influenced both toxin levels and nutrient composition, with nutrient quality peaking in the summer. Risk of Aflatoxin M1 in Milk Aflatoxin B1, commonly found in improperly stored feed, is metabolised by the cattle's liver into Aflatoxin M1, which is then excreted into the milk. Since Aflatoxin M1 is heat-stable, it is not destroyed during processes like pasteurisation or boiling. "Since a small part of aflatoxin B1 in the cattle can convert to aflatoxin M1 in milk, that is why it is recommended that it is better to source milk from the organised sector and aware dairy farmers," Tejbeer Singh later told the media. Aflatoxin M1 consumption, even in small amounts, is linked to liver damage, immune suppression, growth impairment in children, and increased cancer risk. The Solution: Aflatoxin Binders Tejbeer Singh and his team, mentored by Jasbir Singh Bedi, director of the Centre for One Health at Gadvasu, are actively working on a solution. Singh is currently developing "aflatoxin binders"âcompounds to be added to animal feed. These binders are designed to prevent Aflatoxin B1 from being absorbed by the cattle's body, ensuring the toxins are removed through waste and do not enter the milk supply. Aflatoxin M1: A Carcinogen in Milk Aflatoxin M1 is a toxic compound found in milk and dairy products. It is produced when cattle consume feed contaminated with Aflatoxin B1, a toxin generated by certain fungi (Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus) that grow on spoiled grains and oilseeds. The cattle's liver converts a portion of the consumed Aflatoxin B1 into Aflatoxin M1, which is then passed into the milk. Classified as a Group 1 human carcinogen, it is not eliminated by conventional dairy processing like pasteurisation, posing a long-term public health threat.
